## Part 1 — The answer, in plain words

**Short version: no, not yet. But it's much closer than it was two days ago, and the
part that's usually hardest to build is the part you already have.**

Think of your system as a small research firm made of robots.

**The writer robot is good.** It reads the filings, thinks about the business, and writes
a memo that sounds like a real analyst wrote it. It even says things like "I don't
trust my own calculator here, and here's why." That is genuinely hard to build, and
you have it.

**The calculator robot is still shaky.** This is the problem.

I re-ran your calculator today on the eight companies you have saved on disk. No
internet, no money spent. Here's what it says each company is worth, versus what the
stock actually costs:

| Company | Before the fix | After the fix (today) |
|---|---|---|
| KO (Coca-Cola) | worth **72% less** than its price | worth **33% less** |
| CRM (Salesforce) | worth **232% more** | worth **109% more** |
| QCOM | worth **128% more** | worth **78% more** |
| NVDA | worth **59% more** | worth **99% more** |
| JPM | worth **50% less** | worth **51% less** |

The fix helped a lot — the spread between the craziest numbers shrank by about half.
But look at it as a person, not a programmer. Your calculator is still saying "Nvidia
is worth double what everyone is paying" and "JPMorgan is worth half." A real research
desk does not publish that. **The writer robot has to spend part of every memo arguing
with the calculator robot sitting next to it.**

That's the single biggest reason this isn't institutional yet.

**Here's the other thing.** Imagine a doctor who is trained to check your heart. You
bring in a patient with a broken leg. The doctor still checks the heart, writes down
heart notes — and then someone quietly relabels those notes as leg notes and files them.

That is happening right now for banks, insurance companies, and property companies
(JPM, PGR, PLD). The system asks the AI to argue about one number ("what discount rate
fits this company?"), and then the code silently uses that answer as a *different*
number ("what return do shareholders demand?"). The reasoning a reader sees is about
one thing. The math that runs uses another. Nobody would catch it from reading the memo
— and that is exactly what makes it disqualifying. A real due-diligence reviewer would
find it in an hour and stop trusting everything else.

**And three small ones that matter more than they look:**

1. **Three tests are red right now.** Someone is in the middle of teaching the system
   that chip companies are their own category (a good idea — it's what makes NVDA look
   99% cheap). But they typed `TSM` twice in the same list, and in Python the second
   one silently wins. So Taiwan Semi and ASML are still being filed in the wrong
   drawer. The author even wrote a comment warning about this exact mistake — and then
   made it two lines later.

2. **Nothing automatically checks your work.** There's no robot that runs the tests
   when you save. You have to remember. Two days ago you had 263 tests; today 358.
   That's great — but nothing stops a broken one from sitting there for a week.

3. **Your best test only works on your laptop.** You built a great "run the whole
   system without spending money" test. It reads recorded data from the `outputs/`
   folder. That folder isn't saved to git. So on any other computer, ten of those
   tests quietly *skip* — they don't fail, they don't complain, they just don't run.
   Someone else would clone your repo, see green, and think it was tested.

**And one you can't fix with code.** Your market data comes from `yfinance` — a free
library that scrapes Yahoo Finance. There's no company standing behind it, no promise
it keeps working tomorrow, and no agreement saying you're allowed to sell research
built on it. I haven't read Yahoo's terms, so treat this as a question to answer rather
than a verdict — but it's a purchasing decision, not a programming one, and it needs an
answer before anything goes to a client.

**Where you actually stand:** you've built the hard half — the reasoning, the
adversarial debate, the audit trail, the cost accounting, the fact that QC never
secretly edits the memo. What's missing is the boring half: a calculator whose answers
don't need apologizing for, a way to prove nothing broke, and data you're allowed to
use. Boring is also *finishable*. Most of what's left costs zero API credit.

slices via `compute_dcf_from_state`. **Both columns divide by the same denominator** —
the price stored in each slice (`_live_market_from_state`, e.g. KO $87.59, NVDA $200.75),

| Ticker | Old FV | Old vs px | New FV | New vs px | Method |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| CRM | $610.92 | +232% | $385.22 | **+109%** | multi_stage_fcf_dcf |
| NVDA | $318.63 | +59% | $398.79 | **+99%** | multi_stage_fcf_dcf |
| QCOM | $335.83 | +128% | $262.06 | **+78%** | multi_stage_fcf_dcf |
| PLD | $147.98 | +2% | $147.98 | **+2%** | ffo_nav |
| CVX | $150.40 | −24% | $142.69 | **−28%** | cycle_normalized…**placeholder**_trailing_base |

**Dispersion: 304 points → 160 points. Halved, and still unpublishable.** An engine that

### G2 — BLOCKER. The model argues about one quantity; the engine consumes another. *(F3, still open)*

Unchanged from 08-03, and now the strongest single reason to withhold the label.

`agents.py:1263` — `CRITIQUE_SYSTEM_PROMPT` is still **one global prompt** offering
exactly six dials:

```
"parameter": "wacc" | "g_high" | "g_terminal" | "high_growth_years"
             | "fade_years" | "base_fcf_method"
```

`grep -n "cost_of_equity\|plowback\|ffo_multiple\|cap_rate\|nav_discount" agents.py`
returns **nothing**. The native non-FCF dials exist in `ARGUED_INPUT_BOUNDS`
(`valuation_engine.py:28-38`) and are **never offered to the model**.

So the shim at `valuation_engine.py:1961-1967` remains the only live path:

```python
# Compatibility for the committed 2026-07-30 critiques.
if "cost_of_equity" not in ranges and isinstance(argued.get("wacc"), dict):
    ranges["cost_of_equity"] = list(argued["wacc"]["argued_range"])
if "plowback" not in ranges and isinstance(argued.get("g_high"), dict):
    ranges["plowback"] = [growth / roe clamped 0..1]
```

The comment calls it back-compat for one committed batch. It is not — the guard
`if "cost_of_equity" not in ranges` can never be false, because nothing can put
`cost_of_equity` in `ranges`. **Every live bank and insurer run goes through it.**

Two consequences, both disqualifying:

1. The reasoning shown to the reader is about a WACC; the number computed is a cost of
   equity. JPM's argued `[0.09, 0.105]` was reasoned as a discount rate, consumed as
   required equity return.
2. Criterion C2 ("every argued input cites resolvable evidence") **passes** — while the
   evidence was written about a different quantity. Your own quality gate is blind to it.

**REITs are worse.** There is no FFO/NAV shim (`valuation_engine.py:1980-1990`). When no
FFO/NAV dial is argued — and none can be, since none are offered — the range is stamped
`convention` and, correctly, does not satisfy the "expressed as a range" criterion. **A
REIT cannot produce an argued valuation range today.** PLD's unchanged $147.98 above is
that: nothing to argue with.

*Prompt + bounds work is offline. Proving behaviour changed needs one live run.*

### G3 — HIGH. Live classification bug in the working tree; suite is red. *(new)*

The three failing tests are **not** just stale goldens. One is a real defect.

`mas_sector_system/archetype.py` (uncommitted) adds a `semiconductor` archetype —
correctly motivated: `general` carries a 0.95 unlevered beta, which is what produced

Then, 33 lines later, the same mistake:

```
archetype.py:109:   "AVGO": "semiconductor", "INTC": "semiconductor", "TSM": "semiconductor",
archetype.py:142:   "TSM": "general", "ASML": "general", "SAP": "software_saas",
```

`TSM` and `ASML` are duplicate keys in `TICKER_ARCHETYPE`; line 142 silently wins. Both
still classify as `general` — **the exact bug the change exists to fix, half-landed.**
`tests/test_cost_of_capital.py:272` catches it (`AssertionError: TSM`).

**This reaches the numbers.** Traced end to end:
`compute_cost_of_capital(state, archetype=archetype, …)` (`valuation_engine.py:1598`) →
`SECTOR_UNLEVERED_BETA.get(archetype)` (`cost_of_capital.py:199`), where
`general = 0.95` and `semiconductor = 1.40` — a 47% relative gap in asset beta, which
re-levers into the WACC and moves fair value directly. The observed-beta override at
`cost_of_capital.py:248` would mask it, but **`beta` is `None` on all eight stored
slices**, so the archetype constant is the live path, not the fallback. TSM and ASML
are today priced off a 0.95 asset beta that the same commit declares wrong for semis.

The other two failures (`test_us_sector_coverage.py:60`, `test_structural_phases.py`)
*are* stale goldens: they assert NVDA → `general`, which is now deliberately wrong.
`test_us_sector_coverage.py` was partially updated (6 lines) but its `GOLDEN_MATRIX`
wasn't. **Contract tests updated in one place and not the other is the failure mode CI
exists to prevent.**

Also still open from F7: this is **624 lines of uncommitted `driver_templates.py` work**

## Bottom line

**Not institutional yet — and the reason is not the part you'd expect.**

The thinking is done. The writing is done. That's the part most people building this
never get right, and you got it right.

What's left is four boring things:

1. **The calculator still gives answers the writer has to argue with** (G1). Halved, not
   fixed.
2. **For banks, insurers, and property companies, the system reasons about one number
   and quietly does math on a different one** (G2). Invisible to a reader. Fatal to a
   reviewer.
3. **Nothing checks that any of it still works** (G3/G4) — no CI, three red tests, and
   your best test silently skips on every machine but this one.
4. **You may not be allowed to use the data** (Part 4, item 9).

Two of those you can finish this week without spending a cent on API calls. One needs a
prompt rewrite, a deleted shim, and a single live run to confirm. The last one is a
phone call and a purchase order, not a commit.

None of them are hard. They're just unglamorous — and unglamorous is what separates a
very good research prototype from a system a desk will stand behind.
